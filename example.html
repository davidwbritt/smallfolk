A Friday Night in Ostenmoor

A fictional-but-faithful game night using every Smallfolk tool at the moment it earns its place. Every name, secret, and rumor below is real — it all comes from one seed.

Open Ostenmoor yourself → seed “bram-combe-7”
The thirty seconds of theory: a tabletop RPG is structured make-believe. One person — the GM (Game Master) — plays the world: describes places, voices villagers, decides consequences. The other players each play one hero, saying what their character does. When an outcome is uncertain (“does she believe my lie?”), dice decide. That’s the whole machine.

7:40 pm Prep

Sarah is GMing for three friends at 8:00 and has nothing prepared. She opens Smallfolk, clicks New village twice, and lands on Ostenmoor — population 138, on a river — and the talk of the village stops her cold: “Two graves opened in the night and closed again, tidily. Nothing obvious taken. The sexton found boot prints and is not sleeping.”

She reads the red ink — and in ninety seconds she knows the whole truth: Tansy Copperwright, the shepherd, buried private letters with the dead years ago and dug them back up, because someone in the market town has started asking questions those letters could answer. Ned Hollis, the shrine keeper, saw her lantern from the privy and told no one. And suspicion has fallen on poor Fenn Hollis, the innkeeper, “on the strength of a spade and a reputation.” She hits Print / PDF. Her entire prep is done before the kettle boils. Total: eight minutes.

8:15 pm The players arrive

Sarah reads the road-gossip version — the black ink only. The players get the mystery, never the answer. Their characters take rooms at The Crooked Lantern. The sheet says it’s kept by Fenn Hollis, and the house rule is “Songs are permitted; that song is not.” Sarah doesn’t know what that song is either — nobody does — but she says it deadpan, and the table immediately wants to know. The sheet also says who’s drinking tonight: Wat, pointedly not talking to Ned Hollis, loudly. The players notice. They were always going to notice.

8:40 pm Asking around — the rumor table

The players split up to gather gossip. Each time someone works the room, Sarah rolls the d8 on the rumor table. One player hears: “Fenn Hollis owns the only spade with a mended haft, and the prints show a mended haft.” The red ink tells Sarah it’s FALSE — half the parish mends hafts the same way — so she plays it confident, juicy, and wrong. Another hears: “Whoever it was, they prayed after. The grass was knelt on.” TRUE — and it quietly cuts the suspect list to the devout. One real clue, one accusation of an innocent man. That is exactly the shape of a mystery.

9:10 pm The interrogation — the reaction roll

The players corner Ned Hollis at the shrine. How does Ned feel about strangers tonight? Sarah clicks reaction? in the dice tray: “wary — answers cost extra.” But Ned’s card says he wants to be asked to sing again — so when a player half-jokes about the choir, Ned thaws. His red ink says he owes the reeve more than his farm is worth, so when the questions reach the night of the lantern, he lies badly — protecting his own mess, not Tansy’s. The players smell the lie and misread it completely. Perfect.

9:32 pm The knock at the door

It is not a villager. It is the pizza. There is a rule at every table older than any rulebook: the mystery pauses for the pizza. Sarah, who has been Ned Hollis for ten straight minutes, answers the door slightly as Ned Hollis, and the delivery driver — a veteran of Friday nights — does not blink. The character sheets are moved out of grease range; the dice are not, because dice are washable and everyone knows the d20 rolls better with a little marinara on it. Play resumes eleven minutes later with someone asking, mouth full: “Wait — so who else mends hafts?”

9:50 pm The break in the case

A player asks something no table covers: “Who tends the graves? I want to check the north grave for flowers.” Sarah improvises from what the sheet already gave her: the gravedigger is Marrow Gorse, whose red ink says she is silently in love with Ned Hollis. Sarah decides Marrow leaves the flowers — a small tender secret with nothing to do with the crime — and the table audibly awws. Two questions later, someone notices that Tansy Copperwright “wants everyone to stop asking if they’re well,” and the knelt-grass clue clicks into place.

10:30 pm Resolution — and next week

Tansy, confronted gently, hands over nothing and admits everything. The players vote not to turn her in — the real threat is the stranger in the market town asking questions. And the sheet has already left Sarah a road for next week: the shire map shows the market town of the Hundred one road east, with its own Trouble waiting, and one moot-hall notice is still unexplained: a courier nine days late, her satchel arrived without her. Season two writes itself.

The solo variant, in one paragraph

No friends free? Toggle Player view so the sheet hides the red ink from you. Open the Journal, press ⚑ Begin — it writes your arrival at The Crooked Lantern for you — and play the detective yourself, asking the oracle your questions: “Is Fenn acting guilty?” → unlikely? → “No, and…” — so you write: Fenn is the one person visibly heartbroken by the suspicion. When stuck, ✶ Something happens throws a real villager at you. At the end, Export hands you the short story you just wrote with dice. When you finally want the truth, turn off Player view and find out whether you solved your own case.
